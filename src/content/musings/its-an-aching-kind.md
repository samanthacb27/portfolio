When I was five, all I wanted to be was stuffed full of pancakes from the diner down the street. It was a Perkins, which I later came to find out was just another crappy chain restaurant with cheap All-American breakfast items. We had dinner there every Tuesday night, when kids ate free. Back then I thought it was the most magical place in my small world.

The three of us – my dad, older sister, and I – always sat at a booth with dark green vinyl stretched tightly across polyurethane foam, exposed by cracks that grew more with every visit. As I sat next to my sister, with her brown hair that shone like syrup under the warm overhead light and her toothy overbite not yet straightened by braces, I dug my itty-bitty teeny-weeny fingers into those cracks, tearing away at the foam underneath.

Across from us sat our dad, a gentle giant. He towered over us at six-foot-six, despite constantly bowing forward in a restrained chuckle. He lit the booth with his crooked smile and crow’s feet.

We were separated by a dark wooden table, sticky to the touch, covered in a permanent film of syrup, grease, and cigarette smoke. The two of us wiggled and bounced in our seats, banging our hands on the table and frothing at the mouth, like two rabid dogs who haven’t seen a good meal in weeks.

An older lady wearing a dark green apron held her arms out, balancing two trays, one carrying a glass of milk, orange juice, and a large glass of half-n-half tea, the other carrying two stacks of pancakes and an omelet. I always got the same thing: a tall stack of fluffy buttermilk pancakes topped in a downpour of vivid pink, green, and yellow sprinkles and a fluffy cloud of whipped cream.

When we got up to pay, my sister bolted straight to the pastry case up front, carrying pies of every kind: apple pie, cherry pie, banana cream, and coconut cream pies galore. Hands splayed out wide, flattened on the cool glass, she pressed her face up close, breath fogging up and blocking her view. My attention was somewhere else.

Right by the front door stood a gumball machine as tall as me. It was a yellow machine with a large glass bowl on top, holding big fat double bubble gum balls of every color. I tugged on my dad’s denim jeans and begged for a quarter. After sliding the coin into the slot and turning the metal knob three times, a bright red gum ball began its journey, rolling down the winding track of the clear center tube. I followed its movement intensely as it went around, loop after loop, until it dropped into the bottom compartment with a loud clang. If I was lucky, I could get multiple. I would watch as they chased each other down to the bottom, and I would shove them all in my mouth at once. Their sweet flavor would fade quickly after just a few chews, but they would leave my tongue stained with color for hours.

I don’t remember the last trip we took to Perkins. I do remember the day I drove past and realized it had turned into a Picasso Pawn.

I’m grieving a booth I will never sit at again. I’m grieving a time that I will never return to. I’m grieving a relationship with my sister and my dad that will never be the same, as we drift out of each other’s orbits. My sister is across the country, living solo in the stifling desert of Arizona. My dad is always on the move, living on the road in the 40-foot trailer he calls home. Who knows where I will be come graduation this spring. Each of us is living our own lives, chasing our own dreams.

It’s an aching kind of growing.
